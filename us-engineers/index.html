What if, from 2027, no Indian engineer moved to America?

About 100,000 Indian engineers, students and family members move to the US each year (H-1B, L-1, students, families; the models' estimate). I asked five AI models what happens to both economies if that stops.

Where does the work go?

  • About a third goes to Americans: real gains for US-born early-career tech workers, fading by 2050.
  • A quarter to a third simply moves to India: global capability centres and IT firms. This is the biggest US loss: output, salaries and about $27–70B a year of US taxes leave.
  • The rest goes to other countries, other immigrants, automation, or isn't done.

What they agreed on

  • America loses more than India gains: an engineer produces more in the US today (though by 2050 an engineer in India produces about 65% of US output, up from about 27%).
  • No US housing crash: prices in tech cities end up about 1–3% below trend by 2050.
  • India gains even after losing remittances: extra services exports outweigh the money engineers would have sent home.
  • The biggest losers are the engineers themselves: their lower pay at home shows up in neither country's GDP.
  • Only GPT-6 allowed a scenario where the US comes out ahead (+$116B in its best case).

My own run

I took one model (Opus's) and tweaked four assumptions: more US local hiring, AI cutting each engineer's value by 20%, 20% going to Canada or the UK instead, and a smaller innovation loss. Result by 2050: USA −$210B, India +$95B a year, close to the models' middle.

How the experiment was run

  • Each Claude model ran separately with web access; GPT-6's answer came from ChatGPT with the same prompt.
  • Every route was counted once: H-1B, L-1, students (including tuition and living costs), families and US-born children.
  • Each model verified the current US rules and labour-market data, built four scenarios and challenged its own result.
  • The fifth Claude model, Haiku, was excluded: its arithmetic was off by about 130× (675,000 × $18,000 is $12 billion, not $1.6 trillion), twice.

# Independent Economic Research: What If, From Today, No Indian Engineer Moves to the United States?

Act as an independent economic research team consisting of:

* A labor economist specializing in high-skilled immigration (H-1B, F-1/OPT, L-1, employment green cards)
* An economist of innovation, offshoring and multinational firms
* A development and macro economist specializing in India's IT services, GCCs (global capability centres), startups, remittances and the rupee
* A quantitative economist specializing in scenario and general-equilibrium modeling

## Primary research question

**If, starting in 2027, no new Indian engineer or technology professional moves to the United States, and all of them stay and work in India instead, what happens to the US and Indian economies by 2030, by 2036 and in the long term by 2050?**

Indian-born engineers already living in the US stay where they are (test a "some return home" variant in the sensitivity section).

I want your most defensible independent estimate, not a nationalist narrative, an anti-immigration or pro-immigration argument, or a repetition of commonly quoted figures. **Do not assume the answer is a loss for the US or a gain for India. Either country could gain, lose or see almost no net change, and you must test each possibility.**

Primary outputs:

1. **Change in US nominal GDP in 2030, 2036 and 2050** versus a baseline where current trends continue (USD billions and %), with a justified range.
2. **Change in India's nominal GDP in 2030, 2036 and 2050** versus the same baseline (USD billions and %), with a justified range.
3. **Who gains and who loses inside each country** (for example: US-born tech workers, US tech firms, US housing markets in tech hubs; Indian engineers, Indian IT firms and GCCs, remittance-receiving households, Indian states).

## 1. Starting point: verify today's facts first

Verify at primary sources (Federal Register, USCIS, White House, Department of State, Department of Labor, BLS, RBI, NASSCOM, India's Ministry of Statistics, IMF) and summarize in short, neutral tables:

**US policy and labor market (as of October 11, 2026)**
* Which high-skilled immigration rules are actually in force (for example the September 2025 proclamation adding a $100,000 payment to certain new H-1B petitions, wage-level or lottery changes, OPT changes, and any later rules), including court challenges
* Unemployment rates for US software developers, computer occupations and recent computer-science graduates, and recent tech layoffs and hiring trends
* How many new Indian engineers would have moved to the US each year under current trends (new H-1B approvals, F-1 students in STEM, OPT, L-1, employment green cards)

**India today**
* The number, headcount and growth of GCCs in India, and what work they do
* The size of India's IT-services exports and software/services exports
* Startup funding and venture capital available in India in 2025–2026
* Annual engineering and IT graduates, and unemployment or underemployment among them
* Remittances: total inflows, the US share (RBI remittance survey), and how much comes from tech workers

**Baseline**
* Use the latest IMF World Economic Outlook actually published by October 11, 2026 (verify the date; do not use later reports) for 2026 US and India GDP and the medium-term projections, as your baseline path to 2030 (and extend it to 2036 with a stated assumption)

Clearly separate established facts, estimates from the literature, and your own assumptions. If you can't verify a number live, say so.

## 2. Scenario definition

Define the group precisely and state your definition. As a default, use: **Indian-born people with engineering, computer science, IT or other technical degrees, or working in technology occupations, who would otherwise have moved to the US from 2027 onward** as students, temporary workers or permanent immigrants, plus their dependents.

Count every route explicitly and avoid counting the same person twice (a student who later gets an H-1B and then a green card is one person):
* **H-1B** workers hired directly from India
* **L-1** intra-company transfers (for example from Indian IT firms and multinationals)
* **F-1 students** going for a master's or other degree, including their **tuition fees and living costs** paid in the US and the education loans their families take in India, and their later OPT/STEM OPT work
* **Dependents** (H-4, L-2, F-2), and the **children** who would later be born and educated in the US

Assume:

1. From 2027, no member of this group moves to the US, whether temporarily or permanently.
2. Immigration from all other countries and Indian immigration in other fields is unchanged, unless you argue that US employers would recruit more of them instead.
3. Every member of the group stays in India and looks for work there.
4. US companies remain free to offshore work and open or expand GCCs in India or elsewhere.
5. Each government's policies follow current plans, unless you argue that this shock would itself change them.
6. AI and automation keep advancing. Consider how AI coding tools change the demand for engineers in both countries.

Explain which assumptions matter most and how realistic the scenario is.

## 3. Mechanisms to evaluate, in both directions

For each mechanism, state: **the direction (gain or loss), a rough size with sources, what would happen anyway, and what you can't quantify.**

### United States: possible gains or no loss
* US-born and other workers take the jobs: hiring of locals, wage effects, especially if the tech labor market is soft
* Lower competition for entry-level tech jobs and for graduate-school places
* Political or fiscal effects that policymakers expect

### United States: possible losses
* Unfilled roles, slower projects, lost output in firms that rely on these workers
* Less innovation: fewer patents, fewer startups founded by immigrants (cite research on immigrant founders and inventors)
* **Jobs moving overseas instead:** firms hire the same people in India through GCCs, IT-services firms or remote work, so the work, the salaries and the taxes leave the US
* Lower consumption, lower tax and Social Security contributions
* **Housing:** reduced rental and purchase demand in tech hubs (Bay Area, Seattle, Austin, Dallas, New Jersey and others). Estimate whether this means a local softening of prices and rents, or anything like a "crash", and give the evidence. Don't exaggerate.
* Universities losing international students' tuition

### India: possible gains
* Jobs created in India: more GCC, IT-services, product and startup employment
* Higher exports of services, as US work moves to India
* More engineers in domestic industry, manufacturing, government and research
* More entrepreneurs and senior talent staying, with venture funding now available
* Higher domestic consumption, housing demand and tax revenue

### India: possible losses
* **Loss of future remittances** from these workers, and the effect on household consumption, savings, real estate in high-remittance regions, the current account and the rupee
* Absorption: can India create enough good jobs fast enough, or does it get more graduate unemployment and underemployment?
* Lower salaries than in the US mean less income per person, even if the work moves to India
* Weaker diaspora networks over time (fewer new links into US firms, investors and markets)
* Smaller incentive to study engineering if emigration is no longer possible

**Do not assume that every engineer who stays in India produces US-level output or earns a US salary.** Distinguish the job moving (offshoring) from the person producing the same value.

## 4. Build your own quantitative model

Use at least two independent methods.

### Method A: Bottom-up flows
* The annual number of people affected, accumulated from 2027 to 2030 and 2036
* For each person, split the likely outcomes into shares that add up to 100% across both countries: the job is filled by a US local, the job moves to India (offshored or GCC), the job moves to a third country, the job is automated or not done, and the person works in India in a domestic role or is unemployed
* For each outcome, the value added and income created in the US and in India, using sourced salary and productivity figures
* Remittance and consumption effects
* Innovation and firm-creation effects, with elasticities from peer-reviewed research
* Show the exact numbers, shares and calculations

### Method B: Evidence from natural experiments and comparators
Use published evidence such as H-1B lottery studies (effects on firms' hiring of locals and on offshoring), the 2004 H-1B cap cut and other cap changes, research on firms' responses to visa restrictions (for example Glennon's work on offshoring after H-1B restrictions), immigrant founders and inventors, and remittance shocks in other countries. Cite each estimate and explain how you apply it to this scenario.

If the two methods disagree materially, explain why rather than averaging without justification. Make sure the same effect (wages, offshoring, innovation) is not counted twice.

## 5. Four scenarios

1. **US benefits:** locals take most jobs; little offshoring; India struggles to absorb the engineers.
2. **Mostly offshoring:** most of the work moves to India through GCCs and IT firms; the US loses output and taxes; India gains exports and jobs but loses remittances.
3. **Central:** the most defensible mix on the balance of evidence.
4. **Large shock:** sizeable innovation and startup losses in the US; tech-hub housing weakens; India gains strongly from domestic entrepreneurship and GCC growth.

For each scenario, give for 2030 and 2036:
* People affected (cumulative)
* US GDP change (USD billions and %), US tech-sector employment change, effect on US-born tech workers' jobs and wages
* India GDP change (USD billions and %), India IT/GCC employment change, change in services exports, change in remittances
* The housing effect in US tech hubs
* The main mechanisms and major uncertainties

Do not choose the central estimate as the midpoint of the others.

## 6. Nominal conversion and the rupee

Results are in **nominal US dollars**. Consider how lower remittances and higher services exports would each move the rupee, and show at least two exchange-rate assumptions.

## 7. Sensitivity and uncertainty

Test how the results change with:
* The share of jobs filled by US locals versus offshored versus automated
* India's ability to create jobs for the extra engineers
* The size of the innovation and startup effects
* The remittance loss per worker
* AI reducing the demand for engineers in both countries
* Some existing Indian engineers returning home (for example 10% and 25% by 2036)

Identify the three assumptions with the greatest influence on each country's result. State whether your ranges are statistical confidence intervals or judgement-based scenario ranges.

## 8. Challenge your own conclusion

Before finalizing, ask:
* Have I assumed the US must lose, or that India must gain?
* Have I ignored that US tech hiring may be weak, so locals could fill many roles?
* Have I ignored that much of this work can simply move to India, which may cost the US more than the visas did?
* Have I overestimated or underestimated remittance losses?
* Have I assumed India can absorb all these engineers into good jobs?
* Have I exaggerated housing effects?
* Have I counted the same effect twice?
* Am I picking assumptions to reach a headline-friendly number?

Revise the estimate if this review reveals inconsistencies.

## 8b. Long-term view: 2050

Extend the same scenario (no new Indian engineers move to the US from 2027) to **2050**. State the baseline you use for US and Indian GDP in 2050 (IMF projections stop around 2031, so state your growth and exchange-rate assumptions, citing long-term projections where available).

Consider effects that only show up over decades:
* **Compounding innovation:** fewer patents, startups and future large companies founded by Indian-born engineers in the US, versus more of them founded in India
* **The second generation:** the children of these migrants who would have been born and educated in the US (a large future US talent pool) and would instead grow up in India
* **US demographics and public finances:** an ageing workforce, Social Security and Medicare contributions, and the tax base
* **Permanent relocation of work:** whether US companies move more R&D and product leadership (not just support work) to India for good, and whether India's GCCs become independent product companies
* **Diaspora erosion:** fewer new links between India and US firms, investors and universities as the existing diaspora ages and retires
* **India's absorption over time:** whether India's economy grows enough by the 2030s–2040s to employ these engineers at much higher productivity than today
* **AI:** how AI changes the value of each engineer by 2050
* **Re-routing:** what share would go to Canada, the UK, Europe, Australia or the Gulf instead of staying in India

For 2050, give the change in US and Indian nominal GDP (USD billions and %) in your central case, with a range, and explain which effects grow, which fade, and which flip direction over time. Be explicit that 2050 estimates are far more uncertain than 2030 ones.

## Research integrity requirements

* Work independently. Do not attempt to reproduce estimates from other AI models.
* Do not assume a predetermined result for either country.
* Cite credible research, preferably peer-reviewed, for important claims, with working source links.
* Clearly separate established facts from assumptions and speculative scenarios.
* Show calculations so the result can be audited.
* Do not invent data, citations, precision or statistical certainty. Where data are unavailable, say so.
* If you cannot access live sources, disclose that limitation rather than claiming verification.

**The objective is to determine the most economically plausible effects on both countries, not to support any political position on immigration.**
